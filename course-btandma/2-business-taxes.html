2. Business Taxation

Business taxes in the Netherlands · Global taxation, avoidance & ethics

This section provides an introduction to the principles and practices of business taxation, both in the Netherlands and globally. It is structured into the following parts:

  • Business taxation in the Netherlands: We explore the Dutch tax system for legal entities (ZZP, BV, NV), including legal responsibilities and the personal taxation scheme (Box 1, 2, 3). Payroll taxes, deductions, and fiscal incentives for self-employed individuals are covered. Exercises include computing household tax and comparing tax outcomes for ZZP vs BV, including the BV's compulsory manager salary.
  • Global taxation, avoidance, evasion, and ethics: We explore how international tax rules and business location affect tax outcomes. Key examples like Starbucks and the Irish–Dutch sandwich illustrate the boundary between legal avoidance and illegal evasion. Insights from Piketty and Saez highlight how tax policy influences inequality and wealth concentration.

Business Taxes in the Netherlands

The Netherlands offers a competitive and transparent tax environment designed to support real economic activity. In recent years, reforms have shifted the focus from being a tax-friendly conduit to promoting substance-based business operations.

We consider four main business types: ZZP, VOF, BV, and NV, each with distinct legal and tax characteristics. Choosing the right form is a strategic decision that affects liability, tax obligations, and long-term planning.

Business structures:

  • Non-legal entities: ZZP (sole proprietor), VOF (general partnership), Maatschap (professional collaboration) → no legal personality; owners are personally liable for business debts.
  • Legal entities: BV (private limited company), NV (public limited company) → have legal personality; liability is limited to the capital invested.

Understanding the Dutch tax system—including payroll taxes and the personal income tax boxes (Box 1, 2, 3)—is essential for navigating compliance and optimizing tax outcomes. We explore how these elements interact, especially when comparing tax strategies between ZZP and BV structures.

2.1. Legal Entity

Legal entity ("Rechtspersoon"):

  • A legal entity recognized by law as separate from its owners.
  • Can own assets, enter contracts, and be sued independently.
  • Offers legal and financial separation between business and personal affairs.
  • Limited liability: owners are only liable up to the amount they invested.

Contrast with non-legal entities ("Natuurlijke Persoon"):

  • Owners are personally liable for all business obligations.
  • No separation between personal and business assets.

2.2. Business Types in the Netherlands

Non-Legal Entities ("Natuurlijke Persoon")

ZZP (Sole Proprietorship / Eenmanszaak)

  • "Zelfstandige zonder personeel" (≈ independent without employees).
  • In 2025, over 1.6 million registered self-employed individuals.
  • This is the most common business form in the Netherlands, especially among freelancers and small entrepreneurs.
  • The owner is personally liable for all business debts and obligations.

VOF (General Partnership)

  • "Vennootschap onder Firma" (≈ partnership under firm).
  • In 2025, around 170 thousand VOF registrations.
  • A business form where two or more individuals jointly operate a company under a shared name. Common among small businesses and family-run enterprises.
  • All partners are jointly liable for the business's debts and obligations — meaning each partner can be held responsible for the full amount.

Maatschap (Professional Collaboration)

  • In 2025, around 32 thousand Maatschap registrations.
  • A business form where two or more individuals collaborate professionally, often in fields like law, healthcare, or accounting.
  • Common among self-employed professionals who want to work together while maintaining individual responsibility.
  • Partners are personally liable for their own actions and, in some cases, for shared obligations — but unlike a VOF, liability is typically proportional to each partner's share (except for tax liabilities, where every partner is fully responsible).

Legal Entities

BV (Private Limited Liability Company)

  • "Besloten Vennootschap" (≈ closed company).
  • In 2025, there are around 500 thousand active BV registrations in the Netherlands.
  • A BV is a legal entity with limited liability, commonly used by small to medium-sized businesses and startups aiming for growth.
  • Shareholders are only liable up to the amount of their investment, and the company itself is taxed separately under corporate income tax.
  • A BV requires a minimum director's salary and is subject to stricter administrative obligations than a ZZP or VOF.

NV (Public Limited Liability Company)

  • "Naamloze Vennootschap" (≈ nameless or anonymous corporation).
  • In 2025, around 900 NVs were registered in the Netherlands.
  • An NV is a legal entity primarily used for larger businesses, especially those seeking to raise capital through public or private share issuance.
  • Ownership is divided into shares, which can be freely transferred.
  • Shareholders are not personally liable for the company's debts — liability is limited to the amount invested.
  • Governance typically includes a board of directors and a supervisory board.
  • NVs are subject to stricter regulatory and reporting requirements than other business forms, making them suitable for enterprises with significant scale or public interest.

2.3. Personal Income Tax in the Netherlands

Dutch income tax (as of 2025) consists of three parts or "boxes":

Box 1

Income from work and home ownership.

  • First bracket: income up to €38,441 is taxed at 35.82%.
  • Second bracket: income between €38,441 and €76,817 is taxed at 37.48%.
  • Third bracket: income above €76,817 is taxed at 49.50%.

Box 2

Income from substantial interest in a company (more than 5%).

  • First bracket: income up to €67,804 is taxed at 24.5%.
  • Second bracket: income above €67,804 is taxed at 31%.

Box 3

Income from savings, stocks, bonds, second homes, and other assets not used for business or primary residence.

  • Flat tax rate of 36% on a fictitious return:
    • Money in the bank: 1.44% fictitious return.
    • Stocks: 5.88% fictitious return.
    • Bonds: 2.62% fictitious return.
  • The first €57,684 of savings or investments is exempt from taxes.

Tax Credits

General tax credit (Algemene Heffingskorting)

  • The maximum credit is €3,068.
  • It starts to phase out once your total income (from Box 1, 2, and 3) exceeds €28,406.
  • The credit is reduced by 6.337% of the income above that threshold.
  • Once your income reaches €76,817, the credit is fully phased out.
  • This credit is available to all taxpayers and directly reduces the amount of tax owed.

Labor tax credit (Arbeidskorting)

  • The maximum credit is €5,599.
  • It increases with income up to €43,071, encouraging work participation.
  • Above €43,071, the credit starts to phase out at a rate of 6.51%.
  • It is fully phased out at an income of €129,078.
  • This credit only applies to income from employment or business activities — not pensions or passive income.

Various other tax credits for small business are available (see next chapters).

Effective tax rates: Because of tax credits and other income-dependent subsidies, the effective tax levels are (very!) different from the Box 1 marginal tax rates, as can be seen in these tables from the Rijksoverheid.

Employer Costs (Werkgeverskosten)

In addition to the gross salary (base wage paid to the employee), employers have to consider the following costs when hiring an employee.

  • Social security contributions — the employer pays for employee insurance (around 18% of gross wage), consisting of:
    • Zvw (healthcare employer's share): 6.51% of gross salary (up to a cap).
    • WW (unemployment insurance): 2.74% (for permanent contracts).
    • WIA / WAO (disability insurance): 8.47%.
    • Whk (Werkhervattingskas / sick pay): 1.33% on average.
    • Other social funds / AWF etc., variable (e.g., "AWF-laag/hoog", Aof).
  • Pension contributions — often partially paid by the employer.
  • Holiday allowance — usually 8% of gross annual salary.
  • Sick pay — the employer pays up to 2 years (minimum 70% of salary).
  • Other benefits — e.g., travel allowance, training costs, bonuses.

Total employer costs are usually 25–35% higher than gross salary.

2.4. Taxation of Non-Legal Entities (VOF / ZZP / Maatschap)

How Non-Legal Entities Are Taxed

  • Entities like VOF, ZZP, and Maatschap are not separate legal entities.
  • Profits are taxed as personal income under Box 1:
    • 35.82% up to €38,441
    • 37.48% up to €76,817
    • 49.5% above that
  • Each partner/entrepreneur is taxed individually on their share of the profit.
  • The healthcare premium (Zvw) is paid personally at 5.32% (up to a capped income).

VAT (BTW) and Business Status

  • Most self-employed persons must charge VAT (BTW) on invoices:
    • Standard rate: 21%.
    • Reduced rates: 9% (food, books, repairs, transportation) or 0% (exports, international transport).
  • VAT must be declared via quarterly or annual returns.
  • Input VAT on business expenses can be deducted.
  • KOR (Kleineondernemersregeling, small business scheme) exempts VAT duties if revenue < €20,000/year.
  • Occasional income (e.g., a one-off freelance gig) is still taxable under Box 1, as "other income," even without VAT duties or deductions.

Who Qualifies as an Entrepreneur for Tax Purposes

The tax authority (Belastingdienst) defines an entrepreneur based on:

  • Having multiple clients
  • Working independently
  • Bearing risk
  • Aiming to make a profit
  • Having business presence (website, invoices, etc.)

Key requirement: Urencriterium (hour requirement)

  • Must work 1,225+ hours/year on the business.
  • Must spend more time on the business than on other paid work.

Without meeting this, you're not considered an entrepreneur for deductions — your income is taxed as regular income from other sources in Box 1 ("Resultaat uit overige werkzaamheden").

Special Deductions for Entrepreneurs

If you're considered an entrepreneur for deductions you can claim:

  • Zelfstandigenaftrek (self-employed deduction): fixed amount (~€5,030 in 2025).
  • Startersaftrek (startup deduction): extra ~€2,470, for the first 3 out of 5 years as an entrepreneur.
  • MKB-winstvrijstelling (SME exemption): 12.7% of profit after other deductions is tax-free.
  • Lower contribution to social security: only need to pay 5.32% instead of 6.57% for Zvw (healthcare share). No insurance for other social security.

These deductions can reduce your taxable income significantly.

ZZP'ers vs Employees: Lower Costs, Less Security

  • Zvw (healthcare premium): employee — paid by employer (6.57%); ZZP'er — paid by self (5.32%).
  • Sick pay: employee — employer pays up to 2 years; ZZP'er — must insure privately or go without.
  • Disability insurance: employee — covered under WIA; ZZP'er — private insurance (optional & expensive).
  • Unemployment benefit: employee — covered under WW; ZZP'er — not insured unless arranged privately.
  • Pension: employee — often partly paid by employer; ZZP'er — must save independently.
  • Paid holidays & leave: employee — continued income; ZZP'er — no income when not working.

2.5. Taxation of Private Legal Entities (BV)

Legal & Structural Features of a BV

  • Legal form: private limited company (Besloten Vennootschap).
  • Separate legal entity: the BV owns assets and liabilities — not the individual.
  • Limited liability: shareholders are not personally liable for company debts.
  • Governance: at least one director (often the DGA, Directeur-grootaandeelhouder — director-major shareholder).
  • Start-up & compliance: notarial deed required; Chamber of Commerce registration; ongoing accounting & reporting obligations.

Taxation & Income Structure

  • Corporate income tax (CIT, Vennootschapsbelasting):
    • 19% on the first €200,000 profit.
    • 25.8% on profit above €200,000 (2025 rates).
  • DGA salary (Box 1):
    • Mandatory "customary salary": €56,000 or market-based equivalent.
    • Income taxed in Box 1 (progressive rates).
    • Zvw (healthcare contribution) paid personally at 5.32%.
    • The DGA is not covered by WW/WIA (employee insurances).
  • Dividends (Box 2):
    • First €67,000 (per tax partner) → taxed at 24.5%.
    • Above €67,000 → taxed at 31%.
    • Dividends are optional — profit can be retained in the BV to defer Box 2 tax.

Strategy: BV vs. ZZP — Break-even & Profit Retention

  • A BV has higher fixed costs: compulsory salary, admin/accounting; no access to ZZP tax credits like zelfstandigenaftrek.
  • Break-even point ZZP vs BV: around €120,000–150,000 in annual revenue. Above this, the BV structure may yield a lower total tax burden than a ZZP.
  • Keeping profits in the BV: useful for deferring taxation and reinvesting; you only pay Box 2 tax when dividends are paid; strategically advantageous for long-term planning or capital buildup.

2.6. Taxation of Public Legal Entities (NV)

Legal Structure & Governance

NV (Naamloze Vennootschap) = Dutch public limited company.

  • Shares are freely transferable, often listed on a stock exchange.
  • Stricter governance than a BV:
    • Typically requires a two-tier board (management + supervisory).
    • An annual general shareholders' meeting is mandatory.
  • Minimum share capital: €45,000 (vs. €0.01 for a BV).
  • Suitable for large companies seeking outside investors or a public listing.

Taxation of NV Shares Depends on Ownership

If a shareholder owns less than 5%:

  • Shares fall under Box 3.
  • Fictitious return (2025) on stocks: 5.88%, taxed at 36%.
  • Effective tax ≈ 2.12% of portfolio value annually, regardless of actual dividends received.

If a shareholder owns 5% or more:

  • Dividends and capital gains are taxed in Box 2.
  • Based on actual income received.

Strategic & International Considerations

Why choose an NV?

  • Access to public capital markets.
  • Attract institutional investors.
  • Enhanced reputation & transparency.

BV vs. NV:

  • BV = flexible, private, lower admin costs.
  • NV = complex, public, higher credibility.

International equivalents:

  • NV ≈ PLC (UK), AG (Germany), SA (France), SpA (Italy).
  • Not suitable for startups, due to capital requirements and governance complexity.

Summary — Business Taxes in NL

Small businesses (ZZP, Maatschap, VOF)

  • No legal entity: personal liability.
  • Taxation: Box 1 personal tax on income (up to 49.5%); self-employed deductions (Zelfstandigenaftrek, MKB-winstvrijstelling).
  • Best for: small, low-revenue businesses.

BV (private limited company)

  • Legal entity: limited liability.
  • Taxation: corporate tax 19% on profits up to €200K, 25.8% above; personal Box 2 tax on dividends from the BV (24.5% or 31%).
  • Best for: medium-sized companies.

NV (public limited company)

  • Legal entity: publicly traded, stricter governance.
  • Taxation: corporate tax same as BV; Box 2 personal tax on dividends (24.5%); Box 3 personal tax on savings/investments (applies to personal assets like shares below 5% ownership).
  • Best for: large companies, capital market access.

Personal income taxation

  • Box 1: personal tax on earned income (between 35.82% and 49.5%).
  • Box 2: personal tax on dividends from substantial interest of more than 5% ownership (24.5% or 31%).
  • Box 3: personal tax on savings/investments (less than 5% ownership), taxed at 36% on a fictitious return (may apply to NV shares).

Global Taxation, Avoidance, Evasion, and Ethics

Global taxation affects how governments collect revenue and how companies decide where to do business. Over time, corporate taxes have developed from simple levies on trade into complex international systems meant to balance fairness, competitiveness, and economic growth. As tax rates have fallen and capital has become more mobile, countries increasingly compete to attract investment—often at the cost of tax equity.

This section looks at how international tax rules and business structures determine where profits are recorded and taxed. It follows the historical evolution of income and corporate tax systems and examines today's debates on avoidance, evasion, and fairness in the global economy.

Key topics:

  • History of taxation: from early income levies to modern global systems.
  • Purpose of corporate tax: funding public goods and maintaining fair contribution.
  • Global tax differences: comparing rates and approaches across regions.
  • Avoidance and evasion: strategies such as the "Double Irish with a Dutch Sandwich."
  • Reform and ethics: OECD, G20, and the 15% global minimum tax initiative.

Understanding these mechanisms helps explain how tax policy influences business location, global profit distribution, and economic fairness.

2.7. History of Income and Corporate Tax

Early History of Income Tax

  • Ancient times: civilizations like Egypt and Rome taxed goods, land, or people—not income.
  • Middle Ages: European rulers taxed land and wealth; no formal income tax yet.
  • 17th century: England used temporary income-like taxes for war funding.
  • 1799 (England): Britain introduced income tax to fund the Napoleonic Wars.
  • 1803: income tax was repealed after the wars, later revived for other conflicts.
  • 1842 (England): a permanent income tax was set at 2.8% by Sir Robert Peel.

The U.S. History of Income and Corporate Tax

  • 1861: Civil War-era income tax applied to incomes over $800.
  • 1872: repealed post-war; the U.S. relied on tariffs and excise taxes.
  • 1894: a new income tax law was passed, but ruled unconstitutional.
  • 1894: the Wilson–Gorman Act also introduced corporate tax—this stayed.
  • 1909: corporate tax introduced at 1% on profits over $5,000.
  • 1913: the 16th Amendment made federal income tax permanent.
  • 1917: WWI increased both personal and corporate tax rates.
  • 1930s: the Great Depression saw higher taxes on the wealthy and corporations.
  • 1940s (WWII): withholding began; personal and corporate taxes rose.
  • 1950s: corporate rates peaked at 52% during the post-war boom.

Dutch Income and Corporate Tax

  • 1806: the Netherlands set up a national tax system under French rule, taxing wealth and property.
  • 1893: introduced an income-based surtax, aimed at high earners with large capital.
  • 1914: enacted a modern progressive income tax; rates rose with higher earnings.
  • 1940: corporate income tax launched during the German occupation in WWII.
  • 1950s–1980s: top income tax peaked around 72%, corporate tax near 48%; later reforms reduced both.
  • 2025: corporate tax is 19% (under €200K), 25.8% (above); income tax has 3 brackets.

Modern Era

  • 1970s–1980s: Western nations cut tax rates, promoting supply-side policy.
  • 1986 (U.S.): major tax reform cut the top personal rate to 28%, corporate to 34%.
  • 1990s–2000s: many countries cut corporate tax to attract business.
  • 2000s: the global race to lower corporate taxes continued.
  • 2017 (U.S.): the Trump tax reform cut corporate tax from 35% to 21%.
  • 2020s: the pandemic boosted spending; tax reform debates reignited.
  • 2021–present: the G7 agreed on a 15% global minimum corporate tax.

2.8. Why Does Corporate Tax Exist?

Reasons for existence:

  • Raises revenue: corporate tax funds public goods, infrastructure, and services.
  • Fair share: firms benefit from state systems, so tax reflects shared responsibility.
  • Backstop: prevents rich individuals from avoiding personal taxes via corporations.
  • Targets excess profit: captures monopoly or rent-like profits without harming investment.
  • Improves oversight: tax rules promote transparency and better corporate governance.
  • Global fairness: helps limit profit shifting and maintains fair competition between countries.

Corporate tax supports progressivity of income tax:

  • High incomes often come from capital, not wages — especially among the very rich.
  • The wealthiest 10% hold most corporate equity, receiving profits through dividends and gains.
  • These gains are often deferred or taxed at lower rates than regular income.
  • As a result, effective personal tax rates drop at the very top of the income distribution.
  • In some cases, the top 0.1% pay lower rates than upper-middle-class wage earners.
  • Corporate taxes help close this gap by taxing profits before they reach shareholders.
  • This ensures the wealthy contribute proportionally, preserving tax system fairness.

2.9. Global Corporate Taxes

Global Tax Statistics

  • The global average statutory corporate income tax rate is about 23.45% (unweighted) and ~25.67% when weighted by GDP, for 181 jurisdictions as of 2023.
  • By region (2023 Tax Foundation data):
    • Africa: ~27.37% average rate.
    • Asia: ~19.80% average rate.
    • Europe: ~19.92% average.
    • North America: ~25.46% average.
  • Top statutory rates (2024): some of the highest rates globally include countries like Comoros at 50%.
  • Bottom rates: many jurisdictions have very low or even 0% corporate tax rates (often smaller or "off-shore" jurisdictions).
  • Revenue from corporate income tax (CIT) as a share of GDP in Europe: for example, in 2023 among 27 European countries, the simple average CIT revenue was ~3.5% of GDP.

Country Particularities

  • United States: the 2017 reform cut the federal rate from 35% to 21% to curb profit shifting. Complex state taxes and tax havens still hinder enforcement.
  • Ireland: a 12.5% corporate rate and favorable rulings attract tech and pharma multinationals; profit shifting remains a concern.
  • China: standard 25% rate, with lower rates for foreign investors and special zones to spur development.
  • Brazil: 34% statutory rate—among Latin America's highest—plus complex compliance and turnover-based taxes increase the burden.
  • Germany: 15% federal rate, but local trade taxes raise the total effective rate to around 30%.
  • Nigeria: 30% corporate rate plus oil and gas levies; the resource sector dominates the tax base.
  • Switzerland: cantonal rates range 11.5%–21%, offering attractive tax treatment for multinationals.

2.10. Atypical Corporate Tax Systems

Tax on Revenue (Turnover Tax)

  • Taxes total sales, ignoring profits or losses.
  • Simple to administer, hard to manipulate expenses.
  • Can burden unprofitable or low-margin firms.
  • Examples:
    • Pakistan: pays the higher of turnover or profit tax.
    • Brazil: "Simples Nacional" is partly turnover-based for small firms.
    • India and Hungary use turnover taxes in select sectors.
    • Italy's simplified VAT (IVA) taxes small business turnover, easing compliance.
    • US states (Washington, Ohio) impose gross receipts taxes alongside income taxes.
    • Digital services taxes (France, UK) target gross revenues.
    • Extractive industries are taxed on gross output or sales value.

Other Atypical Corporate Tax Systems

  • Minimum / alternative minimum tax (AMT): ensures firms pay a minimum tax despite deductions. Used in the US, India, South Africa to prevent zero-tax outcomes.
  • Capital-based taxes: tax on net assets or capital stock, not profits. Examples: Delaware franchise tax, France's former wealth tax. Provides stable revenue when profits fluctuate.
  • Flat-rate / presumptive regimes: small firms pay a fixed % of turnover or flat fees. Examples: France's micro-entrepreneur, India's presumptive tax schemes. Simplifies tax compliance, reduces evasion for micro-businesses.
  • Industry-specific taxes: levies on production volume or revenue in certain sectors. Examples: extractive royalties, bank levies, digital services taxes.
  • Withholding taxes: taxes on cross-border dividends, interest, royalties. Acts as an indirect tax on foreign income flows.

2.11. Tax Evasion vs. Tax Avoidance

Tax Evasion

  • Illegally hiding income, lying on returns, or not paying tax.
  • Examples: offshore accounts, fake invoices, underreporting income.
  • Clearly criminal and subject to audits, penalties, or prison.
  • More blatant than avoidance, but harder to detect in global cases.

Tax Avoidance

  • Legal use of tax laws and loopholes to reduce what's owed.
  • Often framed as "efficient planning," but raises fairness concerns.
  • Stays within legal limits, but may violate the spirit of tax laws.
  • Common with multinationals shifting profits to low-tax countries.
  • Famous example: the Double Irish with a Dutch Sandwich — used by firms like Google, Apple, and Facebook to avoid billions in taxes.

Why the Distinction Matters

  • Avoidance and evasion both weaken public revenue and trust.
  • Legal avoidance can mimic evasion's effects, especially at scale.
  • New international rules target aggressive avoidance (e.g., BEPS).
  • Without action, the burden shifts to honest taxpayers and workers.

Example of Tax Avoidance: Double Irish with a Dutch Sandwich

Setup. A U.S.-based tech company owns global IP. It sets up:

  • Irish Co 1 — sells to customers in Europe.
  • Dutch Co — receives royalties from Ireland, passes them to Bermuda.
  • Irish Co 2 — owns the IP, but is tax resident in Bermuda (0% tax).
  1. Sales revenue from outside the US in Ireland: Irish Co 1 books €1,000 million in European sales.
  2. Royalties paid to Dutch Co: Irish Co 1 pays €900M in royalties to Dutch Co (for use of the IP). This reduces its taxable profit to €100M → pays €12.5M tax in Ireland (12.5%).
  3. Dutch Co charges a routing fee: Dutch Co keeps a 1% fee (a realistic administrative cut): €900M × 1% = €9M retained. It transfers €891M to Irish Co 2 (in Bermuda). Dutch Co may owe €1–2M in Dutch tax, depending on ruling/substance (or none, under rulings).
  4. Final destination — Bermuda: Irish Co 2, tax resident in Bermuda, receives €891M tax-free.

2.12. Tax Havens

What Are Tax Havens?

  • Jurisdictions offering low or zero tax rates, financial secrecy, and lax regulation.
  • Commonly used by multinational firms and wealthy individuals to reduce taxes.
  • Often have little real economic activity, but serve as booking centers for profits.
  • Enable the routing of income, royalties, and gains to escape higher-tax countries.

How Are They Used?

  • Facilitate large-scale tax avoidance and, at times, illegal tax evasion.
  • Multinationals exploit transfer pricing and profit shifting to shift profits.
  • Real business is often elsewhere—havens host only paper or shell companies.
  • Undermines public revenue and shifts tax burdens to smaller businesses and workers.
  • Pressures countries to lower their own tax rates, sparking a global "race to the bottom."

Global Scale and Consequences

  • Around $850 billion in corporate profits are shifted to havens annually.
  • Leads to $117 billion in lost tax revenue worldwide each year.
  • Major havens include: Cayman Islands, BVI, Luxembourg, Ireland, Netherlands.
  • OECD and G20 reforms (like BEPS and the 15% global minimum tax) aim to curb abuse.
  • Despite progress, enforcement is uneven and some havens resist transparency.

Starbucks, Google, Amazon in the 2012 UK Parliamentary Hearing

2.13. Addressing Tax Evasion

Global Minimum Corporate Tax: Ethics & Fairness

  • Multinational firms shift profits to low- or zero-tax jurisdictions to minimize tax where the real activity happens.
  • Though often legal, this raises ethical questions: firms benefit from public infrastructure, rule of law, and skilled labor, without contributing fairly.
  • This weakens tax fairness, reduces revenue for public goods, and burdens those who cannot avoid taxes (e.g., smaller firms, wage earners).
  • A global minimum tax aims to ensure companies pay at least a baseline share everywhere, restoring equity in the system.

Policy Design & Global Coordination

  • In 2021, 130+ countries agreed on a 15% global minimum tax, targeting multinationals with revenues above €750 million.
  • It ensures that profits are taxed at least once, even if booked in tax havens.
  • The policy seeks to stop the "race to the bottom" in corporate tax rates that has weakened national budgets.
  • Challenges remain: enforcement, loopholes, and resistance from low-tax jurisdictions.
  • In January 2025, President Trump signed a memorandum declaring that the "Global Tax Deal" has "no force or effect" in the U.S., effectively withdrawing U.S. participation.
  • He directed the Treasury to explore protective measures against foreign tax rules seen as unfair to American firms.
  • In June 2025, the G7 issued a statement endorsing a "side-by-side" system, under which U.S.-parented groups would be excluded from certain rules (IIR / UTPR) because of existing U.S. domestic tax rules.
  • Key challenges remain: enforcement consistency, loopholes, treaty conflicts, and resistance from states unwilling to cede tax sovereignty.
  • Despite the U.S. withdrawal, many countries are pushing ahead with implementation — making compatibility and coordination more complex.

Repatriation — Double Irish with a Dutch Sandwich

Where the profits stayed

  • Profits from international sales were routed through Ireland and the Netherlands, ending up in holding companies managed from Bermuda or other tax-neutral jurisdictions.
  • These holding companies were often Irish-registered but non-resident, meaning they paid 0% corporate tax anywhere.
  • The money accumulated offshore — sometimes hundreds of billions of dollars — and was not taxed in the U.S. until repatriated, due to older U.S. rules allowing tax deferral on foreign earnings.

Changes to the rules

  • Ireland closed the residency loophole (effective 2020), making all Irish-incorporated entities tax-resident in Ireland.
  • The U.S. 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) introduced:
    • A mandatory one-time repatriation tax on past foreign profits.
    • The GILTI regime, taxing ongoing foreign income.
    • An end to indefinite deferral of offshore earnings.
  • This forced companies like Google and Apple to unwind the "Double Irish" structures between 2015–2020.

Repatriation and current situation

  • The repatriation tax was set at 15.5% on cash and 8% on non-cash assets, payable over eight years.
  • Firms such as Apple (≈$38B tax) and Google brought or deemed their profits back under this system.
  • Today, most profits are booked and taxed in Ireland or other EU jurisdictions, at around 12.5–15%, and the OECD global minimum tax now limits the benefit of routing income through zero-tax havens.

Summary — Global Taxation

Global tax systems

  • Income and corporate taxes fund wars, services, and public welfare.
  • Corporate tax promotes fairness and limits excessive wealth concentration.
  • The average global corporate tax rate stands near 23.5% today.
  • Systems differ: turnover, minimum, and digital service taxes exist.
  • Reforms like BEPS and a 15% minimum tax aim to curb abuse.
  • These measures protect fair competition between global business jurisdictions.

Avoidance and ethics

  • Tax evasion hides income illegally; avoidance uses legal loopholes.
  • The "Double Irish" strategy shifted billions in untaxed profits offshore.
  • Tax havens cost nations about $117 billion in lost revenue yearly.
  • OECD and G20 reforms target global profit shifting by large firms.
  • Fair taxation ensures companies support society where they operate.
  • Ethical rules aim to balance competition and social responsibility.
