1. Corporate Valuation

Financial statements · Cost of capital · Business valuation

The Corporate Valuation module introduces students to the foundational tools and methods used to assess business value, divided into the following parts:

  • Financial statements analysis: We study the income statement, cash flow statement, and balance sheet, which provide essential insights into profitability, liquidity, and overall financial health. Understanding these statements equips students to interpret performance and communicate financial information effectively.
  • Cost of capital analysis: We cover scenarios without and with debt, tax effects, and the use of the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) to estimate equity costs. The Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) provides a benchmark for evaluating projects.
  • Valuation: focusing on cash-flow metrics like Free Cash Flow (FCF) and investment appraisal techniques, emphasizing Net Present Value (NPV) and Internal Rate of Return (IRR).

We furthermore consider sensitivity to key assumptions and the importance of ignoring sunk costs to ensure robust, informed investment decisions. The valuation skills of this chapter form crucial basic knowledge for assessing targets and synergies in mergers and acquisitions.

Financial Statements

This chapter explores the foundational concepts of financial statements:

  • Income Statement: Summarizes a company's financial performance over a specific period, helping to assess profitability and identify areas for improvement.
  • Cash Flow Statement: Tracks the movement of cash both into and out of the firm, providing insights into liquidity and solvency.
  • Balance Sheet: Provides a snapshot of a company's assets, liabilities, and equity at a particular point in time, offering a comprehensive view of its financial health.

Understanding these statements is essential for anyone involved in business, from entrepreneurs to investors to managers, as they provide a common language for communicating financial information and enable stakeholders to make informed decisions.

1.1. Balance Sheet

The (financial) balance sheet provides a snapshot of a company's financial position at a specific point in time.

  • Purpose: To offer a comprehensive view of the company's financial health and its resources and obligations.
  • Key principles:
    • Dual-aspect concept: Assets equal liabilities plus equity.
    • Historical cost principle: Assets are recorded at their original cost.
  • Components:
    • Assets (left): What the company owns (e.g., cash, accounts receivable, inventory, property, plant, and equipment).
    • Liabilities (right): This includes both debt and equity, in addition to payables to suppliers.

1.2. Investment and Depreciation

Investment

  • Costs are allocated over multiple accounting periods.
  • Acquisition cost paid upfront (capital expenditures or CAPEX).
  • Can be in tangible (such as machines) or intangible assets (such as patents).

Depreciation

  • Accounting cost of using a fixed asset for the accounting period.
  • Accounting value (balance sheet item) of the asset tracked by: Net Fixed Assets = Gross Fixed Assets − Accumulated Depreciation.
  • Depreciation is NOT a cash flow (CAPEX is a cash flow).
  • Linear depreciation: cost equally allocated over the asset lifetime.
  • Asset lifetime is defined by accounting standards.
  • Amortization: term for depreciation of intangible assets.

1.3. Income Statement or P&L

We next consider the income statement for internal use:

Variable Margin = Sales − Variable Costs

EBITDA = Variable Margin − Fixed Costs (excluding Depreciation)

  • Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation & Amortization.
  • Performance measure of business units of different age.

Operating Income (OI) = EBITDA − Depreciation

  • Profitability to shareholders, debt holders, and government.
  • Also known as Earnings Before Interest and Tax (EBIT).

Net Operating Profit After Tax (NOPAT) = OI − Tax*

  • Tax* = OI × Tax Rate.
  • Equals Net Income for an unlevered (= zero debt) company.

Important: The above income statement is suitable for internal use (i.e., for operational managers) who do not know the debt structure of the company, and hence cannot compute the interest rate, and therefore the (true) taxes. We refer to Tax* as the tax level if the company had zero debt.

Operating Cash Flow (day-to-day activities of the company)

  • Examples: sales revenue, operating expenses, taxes.

  • Examples: acquisition or sale of tangible or intangible assets.

  • Examples: dividends, interest payment, equity issue, share buyback.

  • Summarizes the total firm cash flow available to investors.
  • FCF excludes tax benefits due to a levered capital structure.

1.5. Working Capital Requirement

Working Capital Requirement (WCR) represents a long-term need for cash to run daily operations and is defined as:

$$\text{WCR} = \text{Current Assets} - \text{Current Liabilities} = (\text{Receivables} + \text{Inventories}) - \text{Payables}$$

1.6. Operating Cash Flow — Indirect Method

Indirect method of computing operating cash flow:

  • Start from the income statement and adjust for non-cash items.
  • Typically preferred by companies as it is faster and simpler.
  • The indirect method is only used for operating cash flow.
$$\text{OCF} = \text{EBITDA} - \Delta\text{WCR} - \text{Tax}$$
  • Alternatively: OCF = NOPAT + Depreciation − ΔWCR.
  • WCR contains information regarding non-cash items.
  • ΔWCR: change in WCR with respect to last year.

Cost of Capital

This chapter explores the cost of capital, a vital tool for businesses to evaluate projects and make informed financial decisions. The chapter starts by analyzing the scenario of a company without debt, then gradually incorporates debt and its tax implications to provide a comprehensive understanding.

  • The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) is introduced to determine the cost of equity, which is crucial for assessing the return required by investors.
  • The Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) is calculated to represent the average hurdle rate for projects and companies.
  • The discussion then extends to the impact of corporate taxes and (direct plus indirect) bankruptcy costs on the cost of capital and firm value. The trade-off theory is examined, which highlights the balance between the tax benefits of debt and the costs associated with bankruptcy.

1.6. CAPM & Cost of Equity

We will compute the cost of equity ($k^e$) using the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM), which states:

$$k^e = E[R_i] = r + \beta_i \times MRP$$

where:

  • $k^e$ is the cost of equity, which is identical to the cost of capital in case of zero debt.
  • $r$ is the riskless rate.
  • $E[R_i]$ is the expected return on stock $i$.
  • $E[R_m]$ is the expected return on the market portfolio.
  • $\beta_i$ is the beta of stock $i$, formally defined as $\beta_i = \mathrm{Cov}(R_i, R_m)/\mathrm{Var}(R_m)$.
  • $MRP = E[R_m] - r$ is the market risk premium (the extra expected return investors require for investing in the market rather than a riskless investment).

Beta

$\beta$ determines the asset risk premium. It reflects a security's systematic risk, the undiversifiable risk tied to the overall market. It shows how a security's returns respond to market movements, indicating its contribution to a portfolio's systematic risk exposure:

  • $\beta > 1$: asset riskier than the market portfolio, with higher expected return.
  • $\beta < 1$: asset less risky than the market portfolio, with lower expected return.
  • $\beta = 0$: asset uncorrelated with the market, yielding zero risk premium.
  • $\beta < 0$: asset negatively correlated with the market (this is rare). This asset is an insurance against market risk and has a negative risk premium.

1.7. Cost of Debt

  • Companies can choose a mix of debt and equity financing.
  • A firm that has debt is levered; a firm without debt is unlevered.

Debt differs from equity:

  • Debt can be held by banks or other investors.
  • The cost of debt (CoD or $k^d$) equals the interest rate.
  • Debt holders receive interest, but no dividends.
  • Debt holders need to be paid by the firm even if there is no profit.
  • Debt holders do not receive voting rights.
  • In case of bankruptcy, debt holders are paid first.
  • Interest payments are tax deductible (we study this later).

Credit Spread

The company cost of debt (CoD or $k^d$) excluding tax effects simply equals the interest rate of debt, and can be decomposed as follows:

$$k^d = r + \text{credit spread}$$
  • The credit spread reflects the risk of default, both in terms of expected losses as well as the timing of the default (correlation with the market).
  • In addition, the credit spread reflects the costs associated with bankruptcy (which we will consider later on).
  • The credit spread is lower for more senior tranches.

Tools to estimate the credit spread include:

  • Credit ratings attributed by credit rating agencies.
  • Credit default swap rates (insurance against the default of the bond).
  • CAPM (estimate the beta of debt). NB: not often used in practice.
  • Equity prices (distance to default using the Merton model; not in this course).

Credit Rating Agencies

  • Risk assessment: Agencies evaluate debt issuers' financial health, predicting their ability to repay obligations, influencing investor confidence.
  • Market dominance: S&P, Moody's, and Fitch control the majority of the market, wielding substantial influence on global finance.
  • Financial impact: Ratings directly affect borrowing costs and hence investment decisions of a firm.
  • Regulatory oversight: Agencies face scrutiny due to past crises, leading to regulations aimed at transparency and conflict mitigation.
  • Rating scale: Letter grades communicate creditworthiness, from low-risk (AAA) investment to high-risk (C) speculation.

1.8. Cost of Capital

The company's cost of capital is a combination of the cost of debt and the cost of equity. Specifically, the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) represents the company's average "hurdle rate" for projects and is given by:

$$\text{WACC} = L\, K^d + (1 - L)\, k^e$$

where:

  • $D$ is the firm's market value of debt.
  • $E$ is the firm's market value of equity.
  • $L = D/(D+E)$ is the fraction of debt, or so-called "leverage" of the firm.
  • $K^d$ is the effective cost of debt, which incorporates the tax-saving effect: $$K^d = k^d (1 - \tau)$$

Effect of Corporate Taxes

Note that interest payments are tax deductible. On the external P&L, the (true) tax is computed on EBIT minus interest expenses:

$$\text{Tax} = \tau\, \text{EBT} = \tau\, (\text{EBIT} - k^d D)$$

where:

  • Earnings before tax (EBT) = EBIT − interest expenses.
  • Interest expense = $k^d D$.
  • Net income (NI) = EBT − Tax.

We reflect the tax deductibility of interest in the WACC by lowering the cost of debt (i.e., use the effective cost of debt, multiplying the interest rate by $(1-\tau)$ to reflect interest-related tax savings).

1.9. Trade-off Theory

Bankruptcy costs create loss of firm value for excessive levels of debt. The trade-off theory of capital structure states that a firm should take on debt, but not too much, since it increases the cost of financial distress.

Direct vs. Indirect Bankruptcy Costs

Direct bankruptcy costs:

  • Incurred in the process of selling the assets of a bankrupt firm.
  • Examples: lawyers, fire sales, accountants, etc.

Indirect bankruptcy costs:

  • Costs involved as an indirect result of bankruptcy.
  • Could potentially be substantially larger than direct bankruptcy costs.
  • Examples: decrease in sales, loss of key employees, underinvestment, excessive risk taking, excessive dividend payout.

Trade-off Theory

  • Leverage is good since it reduces tax payments.
  • However, excessive leverage leads to bankruptcy costs.
  • Trade-off theory: the optimal debt level balances the two effects.

1.10. Cost of Capital in Emerging Markets

The CAPM does not provide an accurate prediction for emerging markets:

  • Lack of global integration of the domestic or regional stock exchange.
  • High inflation or high risk of government default.

The computation of a company's cost of equity is modified:

$$k^e = E[R_i] = r_c + \beta_i \times MRP + CRP$$

where:

  • $CRP$ is the country risk premium.
  • $r_c = r + \text{credit spread}$ is the interest rate in the country.

Similarly, the higher cost of government bond default and inflation are added to the credit spread in the computation of the cost of debt.

Summary — Cost of Capital

Cost of equity

  • CAPM cost of equity: $k^e = E[R_i] = r + \beta_i \times MRP$
  • Emerging markets: $k^e = r_c + \beta_i \times MRP + CRP$

Leverage and cost of capital

  • Effective cost of debt: $K^d = k^d(1-\tau) = (r + \text{credit spread})(1-\tau)$
  • $\text{WACC} = L\, k^d (1-\tau) + (1-L)\, k^e$

Capital structure theories

  • Trade-off theory: balance the tax benefit against the bankruptcy cost of debt.

Business Valuation

This section examines project evaluation by comparing payoffs with costs to guide investment decisions.

  • The business' payoffs are assessed using Free Cash Flow (FCF).
  • The business' cost of funds is measured by the Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC). Since project risk may differ from the firm's overall risk, the project-specific cost of capital may vary from the firm's WACC.
  • To value projects or businesses, we combine the project's payoff and the project's cost in one metric: cash-flow-based metrics are Net Present Value (NPV), Internal Rate of Return (IRR), and the Discounted Payback Period.

Sunk costs—expenses that cannot be recovered—should be ignored, while opportunity costs—the benefits foregone by choosing one project over another—must be factored in. Sensitivity analysis is crucial for assessing how changes in key assumptions, such as discount rates or revenue projections, impact project viability, helping firms make informed decisions under uncertainty.

1.11. Time Value of Money

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow due to the cost of capital and opportunity costs—it can be invested to earn returns, while future cash flows are discounted due to risk and inflation.

The present value of $X_t$ received $t$ years from now:

$$PV(X_t) = DF(t) \times X_t = \frac{X_t}{(1+r)^t}, \qquad DF(t) = \frac{1}{(1+r)^t}$$

where:

  • $DF(t)$ is the discount factor, which represents the present value of a dollar received $t$ years from now.
  • $r$ is the discount rate. For a project within a company, $r$ typically corresponds to the WACC.
Example

You consider buying a piece of land today for $95. Assume it is certain that you can sell the land in 1 year for $100. The interest rate is 10%. Should you buy the land? There are two methods to solve this:

  • If you put your money in the bank account, you get $95 \times (1+10\%) = 104.5$, which is larger than 100 (do not buy the land).
  • The $100 tomorrow is worth $100/(1+10\%) = 90.9$ dollars today. In other words: $-PV(100) + 95 = -4.1 < 0$ (do not buy the land).

1.12. Net Present Value

We next evaluate a project based on cash flows. A business proposal will raise the firm's value if the present value (PV) of its discounted free cash flows is positive.

Free cash flows of MENSAM:

Problem: cash flows in the future cannot be compared to cash flows today! We need to express future dollars in today's dollars before summing them up.

The Net Present Value (NPV) of a stream of free cash flows $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_T$:

$$NPV = \sum_t PV(X_t) = \sum_t DF(t) \times X_t = \sum_t \frac{X_t}{(1+r)^t}$$

A project or business should be undertaken if:

$$NPV > 0$$

1.13. Internal Rate of Return

The Internal Rate of Return (IRR) is the discount rate $r$ at which the project breaks even (i.e., has zero NPV):

$$0 = \sum_t \frac{X_t}{(1+IRR)^t}$$

A project should be undertaken if:

$$IRR > \text{Business Cost of Capital}$$
  • The cost of capital for a company is the WACC. If a company has multiple projects, headquarters can decide to give different (risk- and strategy-dependent) targets for each project.
  • This expression for IRR is solved numerically.

1.14. Cash Curve & Payback Period

The cash curve represents the cumulative FCF of the project:

  • Provides visual intuition about the FCF timing of the project.
  • The deepest point in the curve equals the upfront or max cash out.
  • The Payback Period (PBP) refers to the number of years required to recover the project's initial investment(s).

The discounted cash curve uses discounted FCF instead:

  • The discounted PBP takes into account the time value of money by discounting future cash flows.
  • The final level of the discounted cash curve corresponds to the NPV.

1.15. Sunk vs. Opportunity Costs

It is important to consider which costs to include in the business plan:

Sunk costs

  • Costs that are identical across scenarios (including the outside option).
  • They could be paid or still have to be paid.
  • Do not consider sunk costs in the business plan!

Opportunity costs

  • Lost benefit due to undertaking the project.
  • Could result in no cash flow when the project is undertaken.
  • Consider them as a cost in the business plan!

1.16. Valuing Perpetual Cash Flows

To value a cash flow in perpetuity with a growth rate $g$ and a discount rate $r$, we use the standard perpetuity formula. Specifically, the Net Present Value (NPV) of a perpetual stream of free cash flows $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_\infty$:

$$NPV = \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \frac{X_t}{(1+r)^t} = \frac{X_1}{r - g}$$

where $g$ is the growth rate and $r$ is the discount rate.

In business planning, it is common to forecast free cash flows (FCF) explicitly for the first several years (up to year $N$) and then assume a perpetual, steadily growing cash flow after that. The present value of all cash flows from year $N{+}1$ onward is called the terminal value of the project.

  • $NPV = \sum_t \frac{FCF_t}{(1+r)^t}$  ($r$ = project cost of capital; check: $NPV > 0$)

  • Opportunity costs: lost benefit due to undertaking the project (do consider).
  • Sunk costs: costs identical across scenarios (don't consider).
